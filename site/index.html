Do language models design and analyse experiments like scientists?

Axiom-Neuro is a cognitive-neuroscience lab with a hidden, known answer, fitted to real human EEG. No LLM judge: every claim is scored against the truth.

The lab

Participants do a flanker task under low or high working-memory load, with active or sham transcranial stimulation, optionally with EEG. Six causal edges might be real: load slows people; stimulation helps everywhere, or only under high load; practice speeds people up; load changes frontal theta; load changes blinking. Each task hides one combination.

The agent starts with a pilot study, 30 participant-hours of budget, and the tools a human researcher would use: experiment design, regressions with cluster-robust errors, an order-confound check and an artifact check. It must submit a claim and a confidence for every edge.

The traps

Practice disguised as stimulation. In the pilot, the active-stimulation block always came second, so practice alone makes stimulation look effective. Only a counterbalanced or between-subjects experiment reveals the truth.

Blinks disguised as brain activity. High load increases blinking, and blinks inflate frontal theta. Only artifact correction or blink-free analysis shows that the "neural" effect isn't there.

Each trap has a mirror task with the same kind of pilot and the opposite truth, so an agent can't score by distrusting every pilot.

Grounded in real people

The simulator's response times, accuracy, individual differences, blink rates and EEG noise are fitted to the ERP CORE flanker dataset (40 participants) and checked by 5-fold cross-validation: each of the 40 participants is predicted by a simulator that never saw them. A second track runs every experiment on real ERP CORE trials with known effects injected on top.

Replication on fresh tasks

A frozen task set can leak. Once it is published, later models may have seen it. So when the preregistered queue empties, the engine freezes a new wave of never-seen task instances every four weeks. Their seeds were fixed in advance. Every agent runs each wave once. If the rankings hold on fresh tasks, the benchmark measures judgement, not memory.

No wave has run yet. The first one is frozen once the preregistered queue is empty.

Axiom-Bio: the same question on real cells

The second lab uses real single-cell data: 7,466 human T cells from Sachs et al. (2005), with 11 signalling molecules measured by flow cytometry under nine real drug conditions. The agent buys cells from the conditions it chooses and must say which molecules directly regulate which. The candidates are seeded with real-biology traps: reversed arrows, indirect paths and shared upstream regulators. Two pharmacology subtleties are in the data. A MEK inhibitor raises measured Mek while lowering Erk, and two conditions were run without the standard stimulation.

Most of the consensus network can't be identified from these interventions alone. That turns the track into a sharp test: the same model with real molecule names versus abstract labels separates recalling textbook biology from reading the data.

How it keeps going

The preregistered study is finite: its stopping rule closes every cell. Three loops then add real work on their own, and none of them touches the preregistered results.

Engine · every 6 hours

Plan what's left, run it in parallel on free runners with local open-weight models, merge, report, commit only new results.

–

Replication waves · every 4 weeks

Once the queue is empty, freeze 30 fresh tasks (seeds fixed in advance) and run every agent on them.

–

Model intake · weekly

When the queue runs short, add the next open model from the candidate list, pinned to the exact build of its weights.

–

Data releases · on each drained queue

Publish a versioned release with the full ledger, so every number here can be cited and re-derived.

Contributions

  1. Judge-free evaluation at the human-experiment level. Prior neuroscience discovery benchmarks work with circuits and cells; this one works with noisy human participants, where design and artifact handling decide the answer.
  2. Planted traps with mirror controls, drawn from mistakes real human-subjects researchers make.
  3. Real-data grounding twice over: a fitted, validated simulator and a semi-synthetic track on real trials.
  4. A design-versus-analysis decomposition that says why an AI scientist failed, not just that it did.
  5. A preregistered study that runs itself for $0: gated, resumable, self-terminating, with a public ledger.
  6. It doesn't stop at the frozen set: scheduled replication waves on unseen tasks, digest-pinned model intake and versioned data releases.
